It has been February for four months now. I’m somewhat confused that it’s 26° in February and I would be taking up my time worrying about climate change and this year’s harvest if I didn’t have a hole in my back. Well, less of a hole than there was. Regularly folk lean down to it, peer into my lumbar abyss and ooo and aaa at how unholelike my hole is becoming.

*Content warnings for discussion of surgery, pain, wounds. No pictures of anything, even I ain't seen those yet.*

On the 26th of February (I am writing this on the 119th of February) I was chauffeured to Nottingham City Hospital where I gracefully walked into an operating theatre. That is a lie. By the time I was in the theatre’s green room¹ I was dressed up in a sensual ensemble of hospital gowns having spent the last hour sobbing in various waiting rooms.

The morning had been spent in the admissions ward, a long room full of curtains, comfy chairs, doctors, nurses, friends, relatives and increasingly scantily clad patients. One by one we were met by our surgeons (mine gave me a lovely back massage) and then left to wait to be dolled up in stockings, gowns and offensive knickers.

I was okay waiting at first. I was reading *Old Man’s War* by John Scalzi. A favourite book of mine and a surprisingly apt one. As I waited for a medical procedure so did John Perry². Whilst John was going into his procedure blind, I was simply squinting. I knew I wasn’t going to come out of it green and sexy but that’s all I was certain of.

I knew they were scooping out my dead cancerous lump that’s not a dead cancerous lump, that’s my wife — but until the surgeons had their hands all up in my back hole³ they didn’t really know what they would be able to do with regards to closing it back up. I could come out with a very tight closure, some sutures and a hole, or maybe they’d really want to get their lunch and just leave it all open and go back in after a pint and a bag of crisps.

So I sat and read my book in my silly stockings as the hours ticked by. I couldn’t eat and the little cups of water did nothing to stave off a headache. By the time noon arrived the powers of anxiety and hanger joined forces and I had a little cry. By the time the porter arrived I was sobbing.

I could pretend I was brave, I was a strong woman heading to her surgery. I could lie and say I left Kaz in the waiting room with a wave and a pithy comment about letting me know if the pope dies. But that wasn’t what happened. I wasn’t even embarrassed that I was a sobbing mess shuffling down the hall — I was terrified. No matter how much I trusted the surgeon and the hospital, how much my Human Brain was ready for the surgery, I couldn’t help it. I was a scared child who wanted her mom.

**Myself being very cheery in the admissions lounge.**

Between the admissions lounge and the theatre I was in another waiting area for a while. I had no phone or watch and through tears I didn’t think to ask the kind porter how long we’d been waiting. Eventually I was led to the aforementioned green room and I finally saw a familiar face — my anaesthetist. A charming woman who kept me happily distracted whilst they got me ready.

I remember telling her about Ted, I remember her telling someone “not with this lady” when they wanted to bring in a student. I was delighted when a student nurse did my paperwork earlier but at that moment I was so grateful for Dr Kathryn for looking after me, knowing how anxious I was. And it was that feeling of being looked after that was with me as I faded from consciousness and remains with me now.

### Waking up

Through the weeks leading up to my surgery I really struggled to see what the healing process was going to look like. I knew I’d be able to get up and walk about but I was unlikely to be able to sit. How would I get from standing to lying down? Would I feel the wound as I moved about? Would the weird vacuum dressing I’d likely have sound like a hoover? Would I feel a constant schlurping sensation? Would I roll over in my sleep and my insides spill out??? How long until I could pick up Teddy and give him kisses on his little forehead?????

When I woke up in recovery, high as a fucking kite, I cannot describe how utterly gleeful I was that I was lying on my back with just a small hint of pain directing me to the location of my wound.

This was apparently the second time I’d woken up. The first time I’d told the nurses “oh I know all about ketamine… from *Helicopter Rescue*” and promptly passed out again, forgetting the entire encounter.

The second time I woke up was when I realised I was lying on my back. I was giddy. I was full of ketamine and fentanyl and heaven knows what else and I made sure to tell the nurse that she was very pretty and that I felt amazing. I hope I was entertaining.

Surprisingly I remember my surgeon arriving (another Kathryn — very kind of them that I didn’t have to remember too many names) and telling me that they’d scooped everything out and were happy with the surgery.

**Having the time of my god damn life, probably listening to an air disaster podcast.**

I was actually in recovery for a good few hours whilst Kaz and my mom waited for me. Sober me would have worried but ket‑me? She was having a whale of a time. She had an egg sandwich? And a Muller Rice? And a pretty nurse? She was being treated like royalty.

It wasn’t until around six-ish when I realised I should get them to contact Kaz and my mom and it was gone seven before I was able to get back to the ward.

In my memory I remember being wheeled into the ward and doing the royal wave at them. In reality I assume I wobbled my hand at them, grinning like a buffoon and looking like I’d been dragged through a soft‑tissue‑stealing hedge backwards.

### Meeting Mr Fartbox

Whilst my wound was mostly closed up there was still a 7cm deep, 5cm wide⁴ hole in my lower back. The dressing consisted of a little sponge inside the hole, a less little sponge over the hole and a load of sticky‑back plastic attached to a vacuum who I fondly named **Mr Fartbox**.

**My first meeting with Mr Fartbox. Fentanyl drip still in place.**

I was not friends with Mr Fartbox originally. Whilst he did a very good job pulling my wound edges together, slurping my back juice and occasionally farting and/or sounding like a coffee percolator, my first conscious experience of the suction being turned on was hands down the most distressing experience of my life.

The beginning of the dressing change wasn’t too bad. I felt a little lightheaded but I wasn’t too sore. And then they pressed the button.

I’m unsure how to describe it. It wasn’t a pain, but the feeling of suction on the wound was so profoundly unpleasant that I felt my senses almost white out. All I could feel was this suction and I was sobbing. Even remembering it now I feel sickened. I don’t know what it was about it but the sensation made me want to crawl out of my body.

It ended up taking several goes and a few hours to make it bearable but by that time I had been well and truly filled up with oramorph and had to be poured into Kaz’s car.

After the first dressing change it got a lot easier. Knowing what was coming meant I could brace myself for the suction turning on and I could actually cope with it. Each time got less uncomfortable but I still hated it even in the last days of the vac.

Thankfully Sarah and Sophie in the dressing clinic are absolutely wonderful, skilled and oh so patient. Sarah, Sophie and Mr Fartbox, all working together to keep my back hole tidy.

### Springtime with a vacuum

So began my springtime haze of going to Nottingham twice a week and spending my time in between carrying around Mr Fartbox and crocheting like my life depended on it.

Kaz was an amazing chauffeur during this time. She’d guide me to the car, equipping me with a packed lunch, a bottle of water, a blanket and a glug of oramorph. Then she’d expertly drive us to Nottingham, avoiding every pothole, knowing the roads’ bumps and rumbles like The Knowledge.

**Being driven through the countryside whilst crocheting is a lovely experience. Especially when you’re full of morphine.**

Some days were good. Some days I was a sulky lump under a blanket. Some days I got to enjoy The Side Effects of having a lot of morphine. If you know, you know.

Slowly but surely I was becoming myself again. I could talk a little more about my back hole without feeling faint, though I wasn’t trusted with measurements until just last week. I was joyous when I could start cooking
